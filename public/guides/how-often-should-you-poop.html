How Often Should You Poop? What Is Normal

By Mark Laurence E. Medina, founder and editor · Updated November 19, 2026 · 3 min read

Educational information, not medical advice. This guide is general information for healthy adults and is not reviewed for your personal situation. Talk with a clinician about symptoms that are new, severe, or persistent. When to see a doctor

There is no single “correct” number. For most healthy adults, anywhere from three times a day to three times a week is normal. What matters more is what is normal for you, how easy it is to go, and whether your pattern suddenly changes.

The normal range

Health services in the US and UK do not set one normal frequency, because people differ. The US National Institutes of Health says only you know what is normal for you. As a rough guide:

  • Three times a day to three times a week is common in healthy adults.
  • Fewer than three times a week is how both the NHS and the NIH describe constipation, especially with hard or painful stools.
  • Going much more often than usual, with loose or watery stools, points to diarrhea instead.

What matters more than the count

Doctors look at the whole picture, not just frequency. Ask yourself:

  • Is it easy to pass? Straining, pain, or needing a long time on the toilet are signs of constipation even if you go daily.
  • Do you feel empty afterwards? A feeling of not having finished is another common sign.
  • What does it look like? Stool form says a lot about how quickly food moves through your gut (see the chart below).
  • Has it changed? A lasting change from your usual pattern deserves attention, even if the new pattern is still “in range”.

The Bristol stool chart, in plain words

The Bristol Stool Form Scale was developed by researchers in Bristol, UK, who showed in 1997 that stool shape gives a useful clue to how long food takes to pass through the gut. It sorts stool into seven types:

TypeWhat it looks likeWhat it often means
1Separate hard lumps, like nutsSlow transit, constipation
2Sausage-shaped but lumpyMild constipation
3Sausage-shaped with cracks on the surfaceNormal
4Smooth and soft, like a sausage or snakeNormal, often called ideal
5Soft blobs with clear-cut edgesLacking fiber; on the loose side
6Fluffy, mushy pieces with ragged edgesMild diarrhea
7Watery, no solid piecesDiarrhea

Types 3 and 4 are the target. If you are often at types 1 or 2, you are likely low on fiber or fluid, or not moving much. Our foods that help constipation guide is a good next step.

What changes how often you go

  • Fiber and fluid: both make stool softer and bulkier, which usually means easier and more regular bowel movements.
  • Movement: a daily walk can help keep things moving.
  • Routine: the bowel is often most active after waking and after meals. See our morning routine guide.
  • Travel, stress and sleep changes: these commonly cause short-term constipation.
  • Medicines and supplements: opioid painkillers, iron tablets, some antacids and some antidepressants can cause constipation. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without talking to your doctor.
  • Pregnancy and age: both can slow the bowel down.

When to see a doctor

A different number from other people is not a problem. A change in your pattern can be. Contact a doctor if you notice:

  • Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
  • A sudden change in your bowel habits that lasts more than a few weeks
  • Unexplained weight loss or constant tiredness
  • Regular bloating or belly pain
  • Constipation that does not improve with diet and routine changes

Seek urgent care if you have severe belly pain, are vomiting, or cannot pass stool or gas at all. Read the full checklist in Constipation: when to see a doctor.
