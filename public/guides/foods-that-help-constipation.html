Foods That Help Constipation (and How Much to Eat)

By Mark Laurence E. Medina, founder and editor · Updated November 19, 2026 · 4 min read

Educational information, not medical advice. This guide is general information for healthy adults and is not reviewed for your personal situation. Talk with a clinician about symptoms that are new, severe, or persistent. When to see a doctor

A handful of everyday foods have been tested in proper trials for constipation. The strongest evidence is for green kiwifruit, prunes, rye bread and psyllium fiber, backed up by a generally fiber-rich diet and enough fluid. Here is what each one does, how much was used in studies, and how to fit it into a normal day.

The short list

FoodAmount used in studiesWhy it may help
Green kiwifruit2 per dayFiber that holds water, plus a natural enzyme (actinidin)
Prunes (dried plums)About 100 g a day (roughly 10–12 prunes), split into two servingsFiber plus sorbitol, a natural sugar alcohol that draws water into the bowel
Rye breadSeveral slices a day in place of white breadCereal fiber that adds bulk and feeds gut bacteria
Mineral-rich waterPart of your normal daily fluidMagnesium and sulfate content may soften stool

In 2025, the British Dietetic Association published the first evidence-based dietary guidelines for chronic constipation in adults. After reviewing 75 randomized trials, it made specific recommendations for kiwifruit, prunes, rye bread, fiber supplements and high-mineral water. Its authors found no good evidence for any particular whole “diet” for constipation.

Green kiwifruit

In a 2021 US trial, adults with chronic constipation ate either two green kiwifruit a day, about 100 g of prunes a day, or took psyllium for four weeks. All three increased complete bowel movements. Kiwifruit had the fewest side effects, such as bloating, and people were most satisfied with it.

How to use it: two ripe kiwifruit a day, for example one with breakfast and one as an afternoon snack. Give it two to four weeks before deciding whether it works for you. You can eat the skin of green kiwifruit for extra fiber if you wash it well.

Prunes

A 2011 trial compared prunes (50 g twice a day) with psyllium. Prunes improved how often people had complete bowel movements and stool consistency at least as well as psyllium. Prunes contain fiber and sorbitol, and sorbitol draws water into the bowel.

How to use it: start with 4 to 5 prunes a day and build up over one to two weeks, because sorbitol can cause gas if you start with a lot. Prune juice contains sorbitol but much less fiber.

Rye bread and whole grains

Swapping white bread for whole-grain rye bread has improved stool frequency in trials, and it is one of the foods the British guidelines support. Other whole grains, such as oats, whole-wheat bread and wheat bran, add fiber too. The NHS suggests adding wheat bran, oats or linseed (flaxseed) to your diet if you are constipated.

Other fruit, vegetables and pulses

The NHS lists apples, apricots, grapes, raspberries and strawberries as helpful fruits because they contain sorbitol. Beans, lentils and chickpeas are among the most fiber-dense foods you can buy. None of these has a trial as specific as kiwifruit or prunes, but they all help you reach the 25 to 34 grams of fiber a day most adults need. See how much fiber you need a day for targets and a food table.

Drinks

Fiber works by holding water, so it works better when you drink enough. Mineral water that is high in magnesium and sulfate has some evidence for softening stool. Check the label: these waters list magnesium and sulfate content in milligrams per liter. Ordinary water is still the foundation. Read more in does drinking water help constipation.

How to start without bloating

  1. Pick one food first. Two kiwifruit a day is the gentlest place to start.
  2. Add slowly. Increase fiber by about 5 grams every few days, not all at once.
  3. Drink with it. Keep fluids up, especially when you add prunes, bran or psyllium.
  4. Give it time. Most trials lasted four weeks. Changes can start sooner, but judge after a few weeks.
  5. Keep a simple note of what you ate and how your bowel movements changed, so you can see what actually works for you.

Medicines and conditions: if you take regular medicines, have kidney disease, are pregnant, or have been told to follow a low-fiber or low-FODMAP diet, check with your doctor, pharmacist or dietitian before making big changes. Fiber supplements can affect how some medicines are absorbed.

When to see a doctor

Food changes help everyday constipation, but they are not a substitute for medical care. Contact a doctor if you have:

  • Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
  • Unexplained weight loss or constant tiredness
  • Severe or persistent belly pain, or regular bloating
  • A sudden or lasting change in your bowel habits
  • Constipation that does not improve after a few weeks of changes
