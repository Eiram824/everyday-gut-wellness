Does Drinking Water Help Constipation?

By Mark Laurence E. Medina, founder and editor · Updated November 19, 2026 · 3 min read

Educational information, not medical advice. This guide is general information for healthy adults and is not reviewed for your personal situation. Talk with a clinician about symptoms that are new, severe, or persistent. When to see a doctor

Short answer: drinking enough water helps when you are not getting enough already, and it helps most when paired with fiber. If you are well hydrated, drinking far more than you need is unlikely to fix constipation on its own.

How fluid affects your stool

Your large intestine absorbs water from the food residue passing through it. When your body is short on fluid, it pulls back more water, which can leave stool harder, drier, and slower to pass. Getting enough fluid keeps that from happening.

Fiber is the other half of the story. Soluble fiber holds onto water, which keeps stool soft and easier to move. That is why health services recommend increasing fluids whenever you increase fiber: fiber without enough fluid can make constipation worse.

What the research says

  • Low fluid intake is linked to constipation, especially in older adults and in people who are dehydrated from heat, illness, or exercise.
  • Extra water on top of an adequate intake has not been shown to help much by itself in people who are already well hydrated.
  • Fiber plus fluid works better than fiber alone. In one frequently cited clinical study of adults with functional constipation, people eating about 25 g of fiber a day who also drank 1.5–2 litres of water had more frequent bowel movements than those eating the same fiber with their usual fluid intake.

The practical takeaway: aim for adequate fluid every day, and treat water and fiber as a team rather than relying on either one alone.

How much should you drink?

There is no single magic number, because needs vary with body size, activity, climate, and health. Common reference points:

GuidanceRecommendation
NHS (UK)6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day
European Food Safety AuthorityAbout 2.0 litres total water a day for women and 2.5 litres for men, from drinks and food combined
US National AcademiesAbout 2.7 litres (women) and 3.7 litres (men) of total water a day, from all drinks and food combined

About a fifth of total water usually comes from food, so the drink portion is lower than these totals. Water, milk, tea, and coffee all count toward fluid intake. A simple check: pale yellow urine through the day usually means you are drinking enough.

A simple daily fluid routine

  1. Start the day with a glass of water, before or with breakfast. A warm drink in the morning can also help trigger the natural urge to go after a meal.
  2. Pair fluid with fiber. Have a drink with every high-fiber meal or snack, and an extra glass whenever you add a new fiber food.
  3. Keep a bottle in sight at your desk or in your bag, and refill it at set times, such as mid-morning and mid-afternoon.
  4. Drink more when you lose more: hot weather, exercise, fever, vomiting, or diarrhea.
  5. Eat water-rich foods such as soups, fruit, and vegetables, which add fluid and fiber at the same time.
Some people need to limit fluids. If you have heart failure, kidney disease, or have been told by a clinician to restrict fluids, follow their advice instead of general targets.

Other habits that work with hydration

  • Fiber: most adults need about 25–34 g a day. See How much fiber do you need a day?
  • Movement: regular walking or other activity helps keep the gut moving.
  • Routine: giving yourself unhurried time after breakfast, and not ignoring the urge to go, helps many people stay regular.

When to see a doctor

More water and fiber help everyday constipation, but see a doctor if you have:

  • Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
  • Unexplained weight loss or ongoing tiredness
  • Severe belly pain, a swollen belly, or vomiting
  • Constipation that lasts more than a few weeks despite these changes
  • A new, lasting change in bowel habits, especially if you are over 50

Severe belly pain with vomiting, or being unable to pass stool or gas, needs urgent care. Read more in Constipation: when to see a doctor.
