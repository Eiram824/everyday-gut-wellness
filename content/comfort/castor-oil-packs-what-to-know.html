A castor oil pack is a piece of cloth soaked in castor oil, placed on the skin of your belly, often with gentle warmth on top. It is an old folk remedy that has become popular again. The honest answer: research is very limited, and many claims online go far beyond the evidence. Many people still enjoy it as a calm, warm ritual. Here is what is known, and how to use one safely.

What a castor oil pack is

Castor oil is pressed from the seeds of the castor plant (Ricinus communis). Most of it is a fatty acid called ricinoleic acid. A pack is usually a flannel or cotton cloth with a thin layer of oil, laid on the belly, covered to protect clothing, and sometimes topped with a heat pack or belt for 30 to 60 minutes.

A castor oil pack is used on the skin only. Taking castor oil by mouth is a strong laxative with side effects, and it is not the same thing.

What the evidence says

The best-known study is limited. In 2011, researchers at two rest homes in Turkey had older adults with constipation use castor oil packs on the belly for three days. People reported less straining and a better feeling of complete emptying, but the packs did not change how often they went to the toilet. The study was short, and as far as we know larger trials have not confirmed the result.

That is about the extent of the human research for digestive comfort. Warmth on the belly, gentle massage, and resting for half an hour may explain part of why people feel better.

What castor oil packs cannot do

You will see claims that castor oil packs detox the liver, balance hormones, shrink cysts or fibroids, boost fertility, or heal the gut. There is no good evidence for any of these. Please do not use a castor oil pack instead of seeing a doctor for pain, a lump, irregular bleeding, or fertility concerns.

How to use a castor oil pack safely

  1. Patch test first. Rub a little oil on your inner arm and wait 24 hours. Skip it if you get redness, itching or a rash.
  2. Prepare the cloth. Apply a thin, even layer of oil so the cloth is damp, not dripping.
  3. Place it on clean, unbroken skin of your lower or upper belly.
  4. Cover it with the wrap or a towel to protect clothes and furniture (castor oil stains).
  5. Add gentle warmth if you like, on a low setting, following our heat safety rules. Oil on skin under heat can feel warmer than you expect, so start low.
  6. Rest for 30 to 45 minutes, then remove the pack and wash the skin with mild soap and warm water.
  7. Store the cloth in a sealed container in the fridge or a cool place, and replace it when it looks or smells off.

Who should avoid castor oil packs

  • Anyone who is pregnant or trying to conceive, unless their doctor or midwife agrees
  • During your period if it makes cramping or bleeding feel worse
  • On broken, irritated or recently treated skin, or over a recent surgical site
  • Anyone with a known allergy to castor oil
  • Children

When to see a doctor

A castor oil pack is a comfort ritual, not a treatment. See a doctor if you have belly pain that keeps returning, bloating on most days for three weeks or more, constipation that does not improve with fiber, fluids and routine, blood in your stool, unusual vaginal bleeding, or a lump. Read constipation: when to see a doctor and how to relieve bloating for more warning signs.
