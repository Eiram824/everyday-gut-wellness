Most constipation is temporary and improves with more fiber, fluid, movement, and a steady routine. But some symptoms are signals to stop self-treating and talk to a doctor. This checklist, based on NHS and US National Institutes of Health guidance, helps you tell the difference.

What counts as constipation?

Everyone’s normal is different. Anywhere from three times a day to three times a week can be healthy. You are likely constipated if you have had fewer than three bowel movements in the past week, or noticeably fewer than usual for you, especially if stools are hard, dry, lumpy, or painful to pass, or you feel you have not fully emptied your bowels.

When to see a doctor

Get medical help right away if you are constipated and have:

  • Bleeding from your bottom, or blood in your stool
  • Black, tarry stools
  • Severe or constant belly pain
  • Vomiting, or you cannot pass gas
  • A fever
  • A swollen, hard, or very bloated belly

In the UK, call NHS 111 or your GP for an urgent appointment, or 999 in an emergency. In the US or Canada, contact your doctor urgently or go to urgent care; call 911 in an emergency. In Australia, call 000 in an emergency.

Book an appointment with your doctor if:

  • Your constipation is not improving after a few weeks of self-care
  • You are constipated regularly, or often bloated
  • You have lost weight without trying
  • You feel tired all the time
  • Your bowel habits have changed suddenly or for no clear reason
  • You have ongoing belly pain or lower back pain
  • You have a family history of bowel (colon or rectal) cancer
  • A medicine you take may be causing it, such as opioid painkillers, some antacids, or iron tablets. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without talking to your doctor first.

Why a change in bowel habits matters

A lasting change in how often you go, or in what your stool looks like, is worth checking out at any age, and especially if you are over 50. Most of the time the cause is something simple, such as diet, medicines, or a change in routine. A doctor can rule out conditions that need treatment and can talk to you about bowel cancer screening, which is offered from different ages depending on where you live.

How to prepare for your appointment

A few notes make the conversation faster and more useful:

  • How long it has been going on, and whether it started suddenly
  • How often you have a bowel movement now, compared with your usual
  • What your stool looks like (the 7-type Bristol Stool Chart is a common way to describe it)
  • Any other symptoms: pain, bloating, blood, weight change, tiredness
  • Every medicine and supplement you take, including over-the-counter laxatives
  • What you have tried, such as more fiber or fluid, and whether it helped

A simple daily log for one to two weeks before your appointment can show patterns that are hard to remember on the spot.

Self-care for everyday constipation

If none of the warning signs above apply, these habits help most people:

  • Increase fiber gradually toward about 25–34 g a day. See How much fiber do you need a day?
  • Drink enough fluid, especially when adding fiber. See Does drinking water help constipation?
  • Move every day, even a short walk after meals.
  • Keep a routine: allow unhurried time after breakfast and do not ignore the urge to go.
  • Try a footstool so your knees are above your hips on the toilet, which can make passing stool easier.

A pharmacist can advise on short-term laxatives if diet changes are not enough. Children, pregnant people, and anyone with an existing medical condition should get advice before using one.
